# Close loops, size days, shrink first steps

The strongest evidence says Presense should do three things. First, get items out of the head with almost no effort. Second, turn only the items that matter into a concrete "next step, plus when and where". Third, make starting cheap, forgiving and focused on one thing. It should not promise calm, better memory or a cure for procrastination. Two findings drive the design. Writing a task down did not, on its own, stop it intruding on thought in the one study that tested this; committing to a specific how, when and where plan did ([Masicampo & Baumeister 2011](https://users.wfu.edu/masicaej/MasicampoBaumeister2011JPSP.pdf)). And whole procrastination treatment programmes achieve only small-to-moderate effects, **g ≈ 0.34 against inactive controls** ([Rozental et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6125391/)), so a single app feature should expect less. The evidence is strongest on mechanisms: effort suppresses offloading, task aversiveness predicts delay, prompts habituate, and broken streaks demotivate. It is weakest on the specific numbers every productivity app ships with, such as 2 minutes, 25 minutes, 3 must-dos, 21 days or "2 snoozes". The best-known effect sizes have also shrunk. The if-then planning effect is probably a third to a quarter of the widely quoted d = .65. And the classic self-imposed-deadline study has been publicly accused of containing fabricated data. For someone who never writes things down, can't start and sometimes over-plans, and for users with ADHD, the practical conclusion is the same. The app should do the remembering, the time-watching and the plan scaffolding. The user should only have to decide and take one small step. Every specific parameter below is a starting hypothesis to test inside Presense, not a settled value.

Evidence labels used throughout: **Strong** (meta-analysis or several independent replications), **Moderate** (one well-powered study, several small studies from one lab, or large field or log data), **Weak** (single small, correlational or unreplicated study, or a secondary report), **Folk** (practitioner advice with no empirical test found). Where a figure comes from a secondary summary or was not re-verified against the primary paper, this report says so.

## Capture is necessary, but only a committed plan closes the loop

The idea behind a mind sweep is that unfinished commitments nag at attention. The popular version of this is wrong in a way that matters for product copy. A 2025 meta-analysis of 59 studies found **no memory advantage for unfinished tasks**: the weighted ratio of interrupted to completed tasks recalled was **0.99**. It did find a robust general *tendency to resume* interrupted tasks, known as the Ovsiankina effect ([Ghibellini & Meier 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-025-05000-w)). So "your brain remembers unfinished tasks better" is off the table (**Strong** evidence against it). "Things you care about keep pulling at you, and you tend to go back to what you've started" is defensible.

The evidence that unfinished goals *intrude on attention* comes from Masicampo & Baumeister's five studies (N = 618 recruited). Participants who wrote down two pending tasks but made no plan reported more intrusive thoughts while reading (M = 3.00) than those who also wrote **how, when and where** they would do them (M = 1.77). The plan group did no worse than controls (M = 1.82), and intrusions mediated a drop in reading comprehension ([Masicampo & Baumeister 2011](https://users.wfu.edu/masicaej/MasicampoBaumeister2011JPSP.pdf)). Two details matter for design. In Study 5B, **listing possible courses of action without committing to one did not reduce intrusions**. And **plans reduced intrusions but not anxiety**: the plan group stayed more anxious than controls. The release also appeared mainly for goals participants rated as important (Study 3). The two notes rate this paper differently; the note that read the full text rates it **moderate-to-weak**. It comes from one lab, has cells of about 25 people and self-reported outcomes, and no direct replication was found ([ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/51234294_Consider_It_Done_Plan_Making_Can_Eliminate_the_Cognitive_Effects_of_Unfulfilled_Goals)). Presense should use the idea without promising the effect. The design consequence still holds: **capture fills the inbox, and the triage or pick step is where loops actually close.** That step should ask for a next action and a when or where, but only for the few items that matter.

The capture step itself should be almost effortless. Cognitive-offloading research shows that people **set fewer reminders whenever setting them takes more effort**, at every memory load, and the benefit of reminders shrank in the high-effort condition ([Chiu & Gilbert 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11103908/)). Offloading improves performance while the store is available but lowers memory for the offloaded items ([Grinschgl et al. 2021](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/17470218211008060)). People who offload also trust the store enough to be misled when it is altered ([Cognition 2019](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0010027719301076)). For Presense, forgetting the offloaded item is the intended benefit. It becomes a harm only if the store loses items or never brings them back. One lost capture will teach a user to go back to holding things in their head, so offline-first, confirmed saves are a requirement, not a polish item. On phones, speech input was **3.0× faster than typing with 20.4% fewer errors** in English ([Ruan et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.07323v1)). That was a lab transcription task, not spontaneous capture in public, so voice should be offered as an equal option alongside text, not as the default.

For the scheduled sweep, there is **no experimental test of the GTD mind sweep or trigger lists**. The main academic treatment is a theoretical argument with no data ([Heylighen & Vidal 2008](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0024630108000848)). Allen's trigger list is useful as a source of categories ([David Allen Co.](https://gettingthingsdone.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/10/Mind_Sweep_Trigger_List.pdf)), but running the full 50+ prompts daily would be **Folk** practice at best and an abandonment risk at worst. Adjacent survey data show **drop-off is steepest over the first ~15 questions** ([SurveyMonkey, industry data](https://www.surveymonkey.com/curiosity/survey_questions_and_completion_rates/)). Uninterested respondents are the ones who quit long versions ([Statistics Sweden](https://www.scb.se/contentassets/ca21efb41fee47d293bbee5bf7be7fb3/dropouts-on-the-web-effects-of-interest-and-burden-experienced-during-an-online-survey.pdf)). How often to prompt is less of a worry. Across 477 experience-sampling studies, compliance averaged **79%** and the **number of prompts per day did not predict compliance or dropout** ([Wrzus & Neubauer 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/10731911211067538)). Those were paid research participants, so consumer numbers will be worse. The combined reading supports two short rituals a day more than one long one.

The evening ritual has modest, specific support. In a polysomnography study of 57 young adults, **5 minutes spent writing a specific to-do list at bedtime led to faster sleep onset than writing about completed tasks (d ≈ 0.63)**. More specific lists went with faster sleep onset, and more detailed recaps of completed activities went with *slower* onset ([Scullin et al. 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29058942/); [Baylor summary](https://kellercenter.hankamer.baylor.edu/news/story/2018/effects-bedtime-writing)). The comparison was against another kind of writing, not against no writing. The study is not independently replicated, and a widely repeated "16 minutes faster" figure was not verified against the paper. Worry postponement, which means parking a worry for a scheduled later time, beats merely logging worries, but only by a small margin: **d = 0.31 on duration and 0.19 on frequency** across 7 RCTs ([Dippel et al. 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41811-023-00193-x)).

ADHD research points the same way and sharpens the case for scaffolding. Adults with ADHD in one well-controlled study made **much less elaborate plans (d = 1.60)** but remembered and carried out a formed plan as well as controls did (86.5% vs 86.7% recall). Time-based prospective memory (acting at a clock time) is reported as impaired in ADHD, while event-based memory (acting when a cue appears) is relatively preserved ([Altgassen et al. 2013](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0058338)). The weak link is forming the plan and watching the clock. The app should help with both: prompt for the plan, and resurface items at contexts ("when you open the app in the morning") rather than expecting the user to monitor time.

|---|---|---|

| People tend to resume started tasks (Ovsiankina) | General tendency across studies | Strong |

What backfires: required fields or due dates at capture, a store that ever drops an item, daily marathons through the full trigger list, prompts that don't apply to the user ("Pets?"), bedtime journaling that replays the day, a "plan" that is just a list of options, and copy that promises calm or a better memory.

## An honest day budgets hours from recorded history, not hope

The planning fallacy is among the best-replicated biases in judgment research. In the classic study, students predicted about **34 days** for a thesis and took about **56**, and only about 30% finished by their own predicted date. Reminding them of past overruns did not help unless they were made to link those overruns to the current estimate ([Buehler, Griffin & Ross 1994](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.67.3.366), figures not re-verified this session; [Buehler, Griffin & Peetz 2010 review](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0065260110430014)). One account explains why "remember how long it took last time" fails: people's *memories* of past durations are themselves too short, and underestimation disappears for novel tasks ([Roy, Christenfeld & McKenzie 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16187856/); [Memory & Cognition follow-up](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03193294)). The memory-bias account is still disputed by the "inside-view optimism" camp ([Griffin & Buehler comment](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/7576332_Biases_and_fallacies_memories_and_predictions_Comment_on_Roy_Christenfeld_and_McKenzie_2005)), and both mechanisms probably coexist. Either way, the correction should be **recorded** actual times, shown by category, and not applied to novel tasks. **No peer-reviewed study has tested an app that shows users their personal estimate-to-actual ratio.** That core mechanic is an inference, and folk multipliers such as "×1.5" or "×π" are Folk.

Breaking a task into its steps ("unpacking") lengthens estimates and shrinks the bias, but mainly for **multi-step tasks**. It helps less for simple, few-step or distant tasks ([Kruger & Evans 2004](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S002210310300177X); [limits summary](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/If-you-don't-want-to-be-late,-enumerate:-Unpacking-Kruger-Evans/67aa82059dafc832f93ad73057fc061ba1487823)). That supports a "what are the steps?" prompt only on larger tasks, not a breakdown step on every task. One finding reaches across features: **forming implementation intentions made people predict *earlier* finish times**, which worsened prediction bias, even though it also made them finish sooner ([Koole & van't Spijker 2000](https://doi.org/10.1002/1099-0992(200011/12)30:6%3C873::AID-EJSP22%3E3.0.CO;2-U), not re-verified). The "start" mechanism and the "how long" correction must therefore stay separate.

**No peer-reviewed evidence supports any daily task count**, whether 1-3-5, "3 MITs" or "plan six hours". All are Folk. What the evidence does support is sizing the day by corrected hours against the hours actually available. This matters for someone who over-plans. A plan that overflows by design generates daily "failure", and the procrastination literature says that feeling feeds avoidance. Self-forgiveness after procrastinating predicted *less* procrastination before the next exam, with reduced negative affect as the mediator ([Wohl, Pychyl & Bennett 2010](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0191886910000474)). Across four samples, procrastination was moderately and negatively associated with self-compassion ([Sirois 2014](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/15298868.2013.763404)). Both findings are correlational, but they point the same way. Rollover should therefore state facts neutrally and require a light decision (carry, pick a later day, or let go). Silently auto-rolling everything creates an ever-growing backlog that reminds the user of failure every morning.

Streaks are the clearest thing to avoid. Across seven studies, a **broken streak in a behaviour log lowered later engagement independent of actual past behaviour**. The effect was worse when people blamed themselves and softened when they could "repair" the streak ([Silverman & Barasch 2023](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/49/6/1095/6623414)). "I didn't plan today" is exactly the self-blamed break. This fits the goal-violation "what-the-hell" pattern ([Cochran & Tesser](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/251808020_When_Goals_Are_Counterproductive_The_Effects_of_Violation_of_a_Behavioral_Goal_on_Subsequent_Performance)). It also fits habit research: forming a habit takes a **median of 59–66 days** (individual range 4–335) ([Singh et al. 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11641623/)), and **missing a single day did not materially affect habit formation** ([Lally et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674), not re-verified). "21 days" is a myth. A cumulative count or a "5 of the last 7 days" window fits the evidence better than a resetting streak.

Temporal landmarks give re-planning a natural rhythm. Aspirational behaviour rises after the start of a week, month or year ([Dai, Milkman & Riis 2014](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2014.1901)). In a field experiment with 6,082 employees, framing a savings increase around a fresh-start date raised uptake ([Beshears et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8341022/)). A secondary source reports that fresh starts help most after failure and can backfire after success ([Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/ritual-and-the-brain/201811/the-unexpected-science-of-fresh-starts-and-failures), **Weak**). So "new page" copy belongs after a bad day, not after a good run.

For ADHD, time perception is less accurate **(g > 0.40)** and especially less precise **(g = 0.66)** in a meta-analysis of 27 youth studies, and participants tended to *over*estimate durations ([Zheng et al. 2022](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1087054720978557)). ADHD estimation error is therefore mostly **noise, not a consistent bias** in one direction. A personal correction for these users should show a range ("usually 20–45 min") rather than a single multiplier. Time should also be visible, following Barkley's clinical principle of externalising time at the point of performance ([Barkley factsheet](https://www.russellbarkley.org/factsheets/ADHD_EF_and_SR.pdf); expert theory, not a tested UI prescription).

| Habits take ~2 months; single misses don't matter | Small meta-analysis plus one field study | Moderate |

## Starting works best as one concrete step, and if-then plans help less than advertised

Procrastination is not a motivation problem for the target user. Across 691 correlations, procrastinators *intend* to act as much as anyone else (**r = −.03**). What predicts delay is **task aversiveness (r = .40)**, low self-efficacy (r = −.38), impulsiveness (r = .41), distractibility (r = .45) and low conscientiousness (r = −.62). **Perfectionism is essentially unrelated (r = −.03)** ([Steel 2007](https://studypedia.au.dk/fileadmin/www.studiemetro.au.dk/Procrastination_2.pdf)). The emotion-regulation account explains the aversiveness link: avoidance is short-term mood repair, and even an induced bad mood increases delay ([Sirois & Pychyl 2013](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/91793/1/Compass%20Paper%20revision%20FINAL.pdf)). Some of that experimental support comes from the ego-depletion tradition, which has replication problems, so it counts as **Moderate**. A Start feature can therefore act on four levers. It can make the step feel doable (expectancy), make it less unpleasant (value), give a reward within minutes (delay), and put only one thing on screen (impulsiveness). It should keep every guilt surface off the Start path.

Making the action concrete reduces delay. Survey items that induced concrete thinking were returned sooner than abstract ones ([McCrea et al. 2008](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02240.x); **Moderate**, replication status unknown). The specific "two-minute rule" or "just five minutes, then you can stop" appears only in practitioner and clinic sources, with no trial of the rule itself ([CBT LA](https://cogbtherapy.com/cbt-blog/end-procrastination-5-minute-rule); [Stanford CTL](https://ctl.stanford.edu/students/getting-started-tasks)). It is **Folk**. It is plausible and cheap to use, but no study backs a particular length. Timer length is similarly open. In the one controlled study, systematic breaks improved mood and efficiency over self-regulated breaks, and a **12/3-minute schedule did about as well as Pomodoro-style blocks** ([Biwer et al. 2023](https://bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/bjep.12593)). Secondary summaries of that paper disagree on its concentration and motivation findings, which could not be resolved. **Nothing privileges 25 minutes.** The Ovsiankina resumption tendency weakly supports the hope that starting leads to continuing. It was measured after experimenter interruptions, however, not on self-chosen avoided tasks. How often people keep going past a short timer is an open question that Presense can measure itself.

### Reconciling the implementation-intentions effect size

The notes disagree, and the disagreement matters for both expectations and copy. One note cites Gollwitzer & Sheeran's 2006 meta-analysis of about 94 tests, **d = .65**, which is the source of the "doubles your chances" claim ([Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/handle/123456789/10973)). Another cites the much larger 2024/25 update of **642 tests**. According to the abstract, it found effects on cognitive, affective and behavioural outcomes ranging **d = .27–.66** by outcome type. Effects were larger when plans used a true if-then format, when people were highly motivated, and when plans were rehearsed ([Sheeran, Listrom & Gollwitzer 2024/25](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/10463283.2024.2334563)). A practitioner summary site reports more figures from that update: a raw pooled **d = .36 (95% CI .33–.40)**, an Egger coefficient of 1.06 (meeting the published threshold for "substantial" publication bias), and a robust Bayesian **bias-corrected d ≈ .15** ([goalsandprogress summary](https://goalsandprogress.com/implementation-intentions-research/)). Neither note could open the primary PDF, so these three figures are secondary and should not be quoted publicly until checked. For physical activity specifically, the update reports d = .14, against up to .31 in other meta-analyses ([PMC11920387](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11920387/)).

The two figures are not really in conflict. They measure different things at different stages of the literature. The .65 pools a smaller, earlier, uncorrected set of studies. The .36 comes from a pool seven times larger, and larger, later pools usually shrink small-study effects. The .15 estimates what might remain once publication bias is modelled, and it inherits the uncertainty of both the correction method and its second-hand reporting. The defensible position is that **if-then plans work (Strong that the effect is real)**, with a true average **somewhere around d = .15–.35 (Moderate confidence on size)**. That is a third to a quarter of the popular figure, and the practical difference between .15 and .35 is large. The stuck-task note still uses d = .65 as its anchor for "large meta-analytic effect"; that label should be downgraded. A small effect is still worth having here, because an if-then prompt costs seconds. The moderators matter more than the headline: a genuine "When [cue], I will [action]" structure, a motivated user and a rehearsal step. For ADHD, the evidence is encouraging but narrow. If-then plans brought response inhibition in children with ADHD up to control levels on lab tasks ([Gawrilow & Gollwitzer 2008](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10608-007-9150-1)). A 2026 meta-analysis in children (N = 12,957) found **g = .31**, stronger in younger children and in some analyses in children with ADHD ([Breitwieser et al. 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41784001/)). No adult ADHD procrastination trial was found. Delivery matters too. Guided mental contrasting with implementation intentions (MCII/WOOP) reached **g = .47** when an experimenter led it, against **g = .28** when self-administered from a document ([Wang, Wang & Gai 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8149892/)). That supports a short guided flow in which the user forms the plan, over a static tip.

Temptation bundling deserves at most a light touch. The original result of **+51% gym visits** (N = 226) faded ([Milkman, Minson & Volpp 2014](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2013.1784)). The large replication (N = 6,792) found roughly **10–14%**, mostly driven by receiving the audiobook rather than by the bundling instruction ([Kirgios et al. 2020](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S074959782030385X); figures via a [secondary summary](https://mindmorphr.com/temptation-bundling-the-evidence-for-pairing-wants-with-shoulds)). "Pair it with something you enjoy" is a fair optional hint. It should not be a core mechanic.

|---|---|---|

## The stuck-task clinic should trigger on sustained deferral, ask rather than nag, and keep AI on a short leash

There is **no validated short instrument for "why am I avoiding this task"** and **no trial showing that reason-matched fixes beat one generic fix**. The closest evidence, tailored health messaging, shows a reliable but small effect of **r = .074** ([Noar, Benac & Harris 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17592961/)). The best empirical scaffold for a reason list is Blunt & Pychyl's study of 30 aversiveness dimensions. **Boredom, frustration and resentment** were most strongly tied to aversiveness. Personal meaning, autonomy, structure, stress and negative emotion also mattered, with weights that varied by project stage ([Blunt & Pychyl 2000](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0191886999000914)). The planned list (too big, unclear, boring, dread) maps onto most of these, but it misses two of the strongest: **resentment** ("I don't actually want to do this") and **lack of meaning** ("not sure it matters"). Both call for different fixes: drop it, delegate it or reconnect it to a reason. The clinic's value is offering a *relevant* option and respecting autonomy, not a proven multiplier.

Deferral itself is mostly benign. In a year-long field study, 463 of 1,191 snoozed notifications were snoozed more than once. Even for those, the reminder finally came back **the same day in 69.6% of cases and the next day in 20.5%**, and more than two days was the exception (9.9%). The authors propose **2 days as an upper bound** for deferral ([Weber et al. 2018](https://interruptions.net/literature/Weber-MobileHCI18.pdf)). Across roughly 40,000 enterprise users, email deferral was driven by **perceived effort and current workload** ([Sarrafzadeh et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/html/1901.04375)). This points toward "too big" and "unclear" as the most common reasons. The "2 snoozes" trigger in the brief is untested. A better signal combines repetition with elapsed time, so that two snoozes in one afternoon are not mistaken for avoidance.

The fixes have their firmest ground in concrete plans. People who received crowd-made action plans **completed more tasks**, with the largest benefit for **lingering and high-level tasks** ([TaskGenies, Kokkalis et al. 2013](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2513560); **Moderate**). That supports gating breakdown behind the stuck signal rather than applying it everywhere. LLM evidence is thin. In a 10-person study, a GPT-4 planner that gathered the user's context first beat plain ChatGPT on plan quality and confidence in next steps, and the authors flag hallucination risk ([JumpStarter](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.03882v2); **Weak**, no completion outcome). Goblin Tools' Magic ToDo, the best-known ADHD breakdown tool, has **no peer-reviewed evaluation** ([Warwick guide](https://warwick.ac.uk/services/library/using/productivity-tools/magic-todo-gobin-tools); **Folk**). No study was found on over-reliance on AI breakdown or on ten generated steps re-creating overwhelm, so both concerns are reasoned rather than measured. Body doubling is popular with neurodivergent users by self-report ([Eagle et al. 2024](https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3689648)), but its outcome evidence is one N = 26 lab study using consumer EEG ([ASSETS 2025](https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3663547.3759743)). The social-facilitation meta-analysis it borrows from found that others' presence **sped simple tasks but impaired complex-task accuracy**, explaining only 0.3–3% of variance ([Bond & Titus 1983](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6356198/)). If Presense offers co-working at all, it fits "boring" tasks, not "dread".

Adult ADHD CBT gives the whole stuck-task flow indirect support, and a caution. Programmes built around task lists, breaking tasks into steps and sizing work to attention span beat active controls. Safren's JAMA trial had more than two-thirds of the CBT group with at least a 30% symptom reduction, against about a third of controls ([Safren et al. 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20736471/)). Effects against active controls were about **0.52–0.58**, and a 2023 meta-analysis of 17 RCTs reported **0.32 against active control** ([Solanto 2025 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12434339/)). The review summary gave sample sizes of "862" and "883"; these look like extraction errors, since the primary sources give N = 86 and N = 88. The Rozental CBT subgroup figure (g = .55) also carries a caveat that one note flags: it held only after excluding one unfavourable study. The caution is that these gains came from 12 weekly therapist sessions with homework review. **No dismantling study isolates which component works, and no app-only trial reproduces it.**

Nudges decay. In the HeartSteps micro-randomised trial, a suggestion initially added **167 steps (+66%)** in the following 30 minutes. The effect fell **about 2% per day and was indistinguishable from zero by day 28** ([Klasnja et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30192907/)). Tailored push messages raised 24-hour engagement by 3.9%, and that also faded ([Bidargaddi et al. 2018](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2018/11/e10123/); snippet figure). People received an average of 63.5 notifications a day, and more notifications went with more negative emotion ([Pielot et al. 2014](https://pielot.org/pubs/Pielot2014-MobileHCI-Notifications.pdf)). Interruptions left performance intact but raised stress ([Mark et al. 2008](https://ics.uci.edu/~gmark/chi08-mark.pdf)). Just-in-time adaptive intervention theory says to intervene only when the person both needs help and is **able to act on it** ([Nahum-Shani et al. 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27663578/)). A user looking at the task they are about to snooze meets both conditions, so the clinic should be **in-app and pull-based**, not a push notification. Tone matters less than choice. A 2×2 experiment (N = 526) found **no effect of autonomy-supportive versus controlling wording** on perceived autonomy ([JMIR 2019](https://www.jmir.org/2019/10/e14074/)), while autonomy and resentment are themselves aversiveness drivers. Real exits with equal visual weight ("Not now", "Drop it", "Keep as is") matter more than polished phrasing.

| Claim for feature 3 (stuck tasks and nudges) | What the evidence shows | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Task aversiveness predicts procrastination; boredom, frustration and resentment lead | Meta-analysis plus a dimensional study | Strong / Moderate |
| Reason-matched fixes beat generic ones | No direct trial; tailoring r ≈ .07 | Weak (small if real) |
| Deferral past 2 days is rare; repeated snoozing is common | Year-long field log | Moderate |
| Concrete action plans raise completion, especially for lingering tasks | Between-subjects experiment | Moderate |
| Context-aware LLM breakdown beats generic | N = 10 usability study | Weak |
| Goblin Tools / AI breakdown "works for ADHD" | No peer-reviewed data | Folk |
| Body doubling is proven for ADHD | Surveys plus one N = 26 lab study | Weak |
| Prompt effects decay within weeks | Micro-randomised trials | Moderate-to-strong |
| Autonomy-supportive wording alone changes outcomes | Several null experiments | Moderate evidence of no effect |
| Adult ADHD CBT (lists, breakdown, sizing) helps | Several RCTs, meta-analysis | Strong for CBT; indirect for apps |

## Seven popular claims to keep out of the product and its marketing

The **Ariely & Wertenbroch (2002)** proofreading study is the classic evidence that self-imposed deadlines help, with evenly spaced external deadlines helping more ([Ariely & Wertenbroch 2002](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/1467-9280.00441)). It is now in serious doubt. On 31 August 2026, the data-forensics blog **Data Colada (post #138) reported evidence that it considers consistent with fabricated data in Study 2**. It listed four red flags. First, an implausibly large effect (d ≈ 2.5). Second, 18 of 20 end-deadline participants had "twins" with identical correction counts on all three tasks and IDs exactly 10 apart. Third, near-zero correlations between variables that correlate at +.63 to +.92 in replication data. Fourth, only 11.7% of self-reported times were round numbers, against 85% in the replication. The same post reports that a **forthcoming pre-registered replication by Hyndman & Bisin in *Psychological Science* failed to reproduce the original findings** ([Data Colada 138](https://datacolada.org/138)). Both claims come from one source, which is an allegation by independent analysts, not a journal finding or retraction. The replication's effect sizes are not yet public, and the authors' response was not in the notes. The conclusion that follows is still clear enough to act on: **Presense should not rely on or cite this study**. The "evenly spaced external deadlines" result falls with it, since it comes from the same study.

Six other claims fail in similar ways. **"If-then plans double your success"** rests on the outdated d = .65. **"Unfinished tasks nag you because your brain remembers them" (Zeigarnik)** has a pooled recall ratio of 0.99. **"21 days to form a habit"** is contradicted by a median of about 2 months. **"Science-backed 2-minute rule" and "Pomodoro is proven"** are Folk and Weak respectively. **"Temptation bundling boosts effort 51%"** shrank to about 10–14% at scale. **"Body doubling is proven for ADHD"** rests on self-report and one small lab study. There are also looser claims: "journaling before bed improves sleep" (only specific forward-looking lists were tested, and detailed recaps went with worse sleep onset), "GTD is scientifically proven" (no empirical test found), "procrastinators are perfectionists" (r = −.03), and vendor figures on streak churn ([example of unverified source](https://appstorys.com/blog-Streaks-Milestones-Habit-Gamification)). None belong in onboarding copy.

## Recommended design specs, build order and in-app validation

Every number below is a reasoned default drawn from the evidence above. None comes from a trial of a to-do app, so each is a hypothesis for Presense's telemetry to confirm or replace.

### Feature 1 spec: mind sweep and capture

| Element | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Capture trigger | One gesture from anywhere: widget, lock screen, share sheet, global hotkey, in-app "+" |
| Capture speed | Under 2–3 s from intent to typing or recording; voice and text equal |
| Required fields | None. No project, date or tag at capture |
| Reliability | Offline-first, instant local save, visible "saved" confirmation, zero-loss target; keep raw audio/transcript, and make any AI clean-up reversible |
| Morning ritual (2–3 min) | Opens with "What's on your mind?" (free capture), then flows into picking today (feature 2) |
| Evening sweep (~5 min) | Opens with "What's still open for tomorrow or later?", then 3–5 trigger prompts rotated daily from: people/promises owed, messages to send, money and admin, home and errands, health and appointments, work/study deadlines, waiting on others, upcoming events, "anything else nagging you?" |
| Sweep rules | Every prompt skippable; "done" visible at every step; no progress-bar guilt; hide a category after the user skips it repeatedly (e.g. 3 times); no recap of the day |
| Deep sweep | Optional weekly, using the fuller trigger list |
| Park it | Send a worry or item to a chosen later time, and bring it back then |
| Loop closing | At triage (not capture), for items the user marks as mattering: "What's the next step?" plus "When or where?" as one committed choice, not a list of options |
| Resurfacing | Anchored to contexts (morning open, evening ritual) rather than bare clock times; the app watches the clock, not the user |
| Copy principles | "Clears your head" (attention), never "stops the stress" or "boosts memory"; no Zeigarnik claims |

### Feature 2 spec: honest day

| Element | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Capacity input | Each morning the user confirms available hours (prefilled from the last similar day) |
| Fit line | Sum of *corrected* estimates against available hours, shown as a clear fits / doesn't-fit line; default ~20–30% unallocated buffer (Folk, adjustable) |
| Overflow | When the plan doesn't fit, ask the user to choose what to defer or drop before the day starts; do not allow silent over-commitment |
| Estimate logging | Log estimate vs actual from day one via the Start timer or completion time |
| Correction | After ~10 completed estimated tasks in a category (product guess), suggest the median actual/estimate ratio per category; show a range for noisy estimators ("usually 20–45 min"); suggestion, never an automatic override; no correction for novel task types |
| Unpacking | Prompt "What are the steps?" only on tasks estimated above ~30 min |
| Must-dos | Optional "pick 1–3", labelled as a convention, not science |
| Planning time-box | Morning planning capped at a few minutes, then a clear hand-off to Start (guards against over-planning; inference) |
| Visible time | Glanceable time-remaining bar for the current task and hours left today; optional gentle cues at 50% and 100% of estimate |
| Rollover | Next morning: "3 things didn't fit yesterday" with Carry to today / Pick a day / Let it go; no red, no "overdue", no day counters |
| Repeated rollover | At 3 rollovers, frame it as a sizing signal ("this might need breaking down"), which routes to the clinic |
| Consistency metric | Cumulative days planned or "5 of last 7"; no resetting streak, and never a "streak lost" message |
| Fresh starts | Weekly review on a user-chosen day; "new page" framing after a bad day, continuity framing after a good run |

### Feature 3 spec: Start, if-then and the stuck-task clinic

| Element | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Start button | On every task; one tap opens a single-focus screen |
| First step | One verb-first, physical action with no decisions inside it, doable within the timer, aimed at the least aversive entry point ("open the doc and type the heading") |
| Timer | Default 5 min; options 2 / 5 / 10 / 25; visible countdown |
| End of timer | "Keep going" or "Done for now", both counted and shown as a win; never auto-continue or nag past the stop point |
| Acknowledgement | Instant small feedback on starting ("started", step ticked); no money, penalties or streak stakes |
| If-then plan | Two structured fields, "When [cue: after a routine, place + time]" and "I will [first step]"; user writes or confirms it; one read-back rehearsal; surfaced at the cue; offered for tasks that matter, not all |
| Stuck trigger | 2+ deferrals **and** 48 h+ since first planned, or 3+ deferrals in any window, or 3 rollovers; "waiting on someone" items excluded |
| Clinic surface | Inline at the moment of snoozing or viewing the task; never a push notification |
| Question | "What's in the way?" (multi-select): Too big · Unclear · Boring · Dreading it · Don't really want to / not sure it matters · Waiting on something · Not sure |
| Fixes | Too big → show only the first 1–3 steps. Unclear → "What does done look like?" plus a "find out X" first step. Boring → short timer, optional "pair it with something you enjoy", optional co-working. Dreading → "ugly first version", 2-minute start, forgiving copy, no audience. Don't want / doesn't matter → drop, delegate, or reconnect to why. Waiting → move to Waiting-for with a follow-up date. Not sure → generic: shrink to a 2-minute first step |
| AI first steps | Only from the clinic or on request; ask one context question first; show at most 3 editable steps; granularity control; user confirms before anything is created; labelled as suggestions |
| Exits | "Not now", "Drop it", "Keep as is", with equal visual weight to the fixes |
| Caps and back-off | 1 clinic per task per defer-cycle; about 1–3 per day overall; after 2 "not now" on a task, stay silent 5–7 days or until the task is edited; rotate wording and offered fix |
| Push budget | Push only for user-created if-then cues and the two ritual reminders (user-timed, each easy to turn off); no stuck-task or "you haven't…" pushes |
| Copy principles | Questions, not verdicts; no counts of avoidance; "want to" and "could", not "should" and "must" |

### Build order

The ordering follows three things: data dependencies, the user's own pain points (not writing things down, not starting, over-planning), and where evidence is strongest relative to cost.

| Order | Build | Why now |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Zero-friction, zero-loss capture (text, then voice) | Everything else depends on a trusted inbox; the effort → offloading link is the best-supported mechanism in this area |
| 2 | Start button with first step, 5-min timer and estimate/actual logging | Hits "can't start" directly, is cheap, and starts collecting the duration data that step 5 needs |
| 3 | Evening sweep plus morning "what's on your mind / pick today" | Turns capture into a habit at predictable moments; short sessions fit the drop-off data |
| 4 | Honest-day capacity line, overflow prompt and rollover triage with a non-streak consistency metric | Directly counters over-planning and guilt spirals; needs only user-entered hours at first |
| 5 | Personal estimate correction by category | Needs about 2–4 weeks of logged data from step 2 |
| 6 | If-then cue fields with rehearsal and cue-time surfacing | Small but real effect at near-zero cost; builds on the Start first step |
| 7 | Stuck-task clinic with rule-based fixes | Needs deferral history; thresholds must be calibrated on real logs |
| 8 | AI first-step generation inside the clinic | Weakest evidence and highest risk (hallucination, overwhelm); add after the rule-based clinic proves itself |
| 9 | Optional extras: co-working signal, temptation-bundling hint | Weak or small evidence; comfort features only |

### What to measure in Presense itself

Because most parameters are untested, Presense should run as its own study. That means an n-of-1 alternating design (feature on for a week, off for a week) while the builder is the only user, and micro-randomised tests once others join.

| Feature | Primary measure | Supporting measures and experiments |
|---|---|---|
| Capture | Captures per active day, and share of captured items that are triaged within 48 h | Median time from gesture to saved; voice vs text share; lost or failed saves (target 0) |
| Sweep rituals | Ritual completion rate per week | Drop-off by prompt position; new items yielded per trigger category (retire low-yield ones); sessions ended early |
| Loop closing | Share of "matters" items with a next step plus when/where | Start rate for planned vs unplanned items; optional one-tap "how clear is your head?" before and after the sweep |
| Honest day | Share of days where corrected plan ≤ available hours | Completed vs planned hours; overflow decisions made; rollover count distribution; let-go rate |
| Estimate correction | Median absolute log(actual/estimate) over time, before and after the correction is shown | Acceptance rate of suggested estimates; error for novel vs repeated categories |
| Consistency and fresh starts | Next-day return rate after a day with ≥50% unfinished | A/B test of "new page" copy after bad days vs neutral copy |
| Start | Start taps that lead to a completed timer | Continuation rate past the timer (the untested "momentum" claim); task completion within 24 h of a start; timer length chosen |
| If-then | Start within 30 min of the cue for tasks with a plan vs matched tasks without | Rehearsal completed vs skipped |
| Stuck clinic | Micro-randomised: withhold the clinic on ~30% of eligible deferrals and compare start within 24 h | Completion probability by snooze count (to calibrate the trigger); reason distribution; fix acceptance by reason; generic-fix vs reason-matched A/B |
| AI steps | Share of AI steps accepted unedited, edited or rejected | Start within 24 h after AI steps vs rule-based steps; number of steps created per task (overwhelm guard) |
| Nudges | Response rate to ritual and cue reminders by week since signup (decay curve) | Notification opt-outs; "not now" rates; ritual completion when the reminder is withheld |

## Conclusion

The research changes what Presense should claim more than what it should build. The mechanisms behind its promise are real. Offloading frees capacity when the store is trusted. A committed next step, not a list, releases attention. Aversiveness, not laziness, blocks starting. Guilt and broken streaks make things worse. But the magnitudes that sell productivity apps have deflated under better methods: if-then plans at perhaps a quarter of their famous size, temptation bundling at a fifth, Zeigarnik at zero, and a flagship deadline study under a fraud allegation. The honest product is therefore modest in its copy and ambitious in its measurement. Capture and Start are both where the evidence is firmest and where the builder's own pain is sharpest, so they should ship first and carry the instrumentation that everything later depends on.

For users with ADHD, the research implies a specific division of labour between person and app, not a separate "ADHD mode". The deficits that show up most clearly are forming elaborate plans, monitoring time and estimating duration precisely. Remembering and carrying out a plan once it exists is relatively intact. Presense should therefore take over the monitoring and the scaffolding, and leave the user with the one thing an app cannot supply: a small commitment they chose themselves. Because almost none of the specific numbers have been tested in a to-do app, Presense's own randomised logs could become better evidence on these questions than anything in the current literature.
